A. Inflation

The pandemic policy response

To understand today's inflation we need to go back to the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, when two priorities overwhelmed everything else: developing a vaccine to slow the virus, and passing stimulus large enough to keep a halted economy solvent. Both shaped markets for years. Vaccine R&D was heavily state-subsidised: under Operation Warp Speed, Moderna received roughly $955m of BARDA development funding, followed by multi-billion-dollar supply contracts.3 The company rode that windfall to a record close of $484.47 in August 2021, before a long decline that bottomed near $29 at the end of 2025; its re-rating in August 2026 is discussed in Section III.4 Meanwhile, physical logistics and services businesses — airlines and hospitality above all — took heavy losses and years to recover.

On the monetary side, the Federal Reserve cut its policy rate to 0–0.25% on 15 March 2020 and restarted quantitative easing (QE): creating central-bank reserves to buy government bonds and agency mortgage-backed securities from the private sector, injecting liquidity into the financial system.5 The Fed's balance sheet more than doubled, from $4.2tn in February 2020 to a peak of $9.0tn in April 2022.6 With hindsight critics argue the response was too large for too long, but it is hard to dispute that it averted a far deeper global downturn.

Fiscal policy was even more aggressive. Six US relief laws totalling roughly $5.4tn were enacted between March 2020 and March 2021, led by the CARES Act ($2.2tn, March 2020), the December 2020 relief package ($900bn) and the American Rescue Plan ($1.9tn, March 2021).7 The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) is often lumped in with this period but came later and served a different purpose: signed in August 2022, it was a climate, energy and drug-pricing law that CBO scored as reducing deficits by $238bn over ten years. Its $7,500 clean-vehicle credit — the centrepiece of the Biden-era push to electrification — was terminated for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025.8

The combination of near-zero rates and unprecedented fiscal transfers produced one of the strongest rallies in market history. From the closing low on 23 March 2020 to their respective peaks, the S&P 500 rose 114% (to 3 January 2022) and the Nasdaq Composite rose 134% (to 19 November 2021).9 The price was inflation: US CPI rose 9.1% year-on-year in June 2022, the fastest pace since November 1981.10

The Fed responded with its most aggressive tightening cycle in four decades, taking the funds rate to a 5.25–5.50% range by July 2023.11 Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 compounded the problem: European gas prices (Dutch TTF) settled at a record €339/MWh in August 2022.12 Markets bore the brunt — the S&P 500 fell 19.4% and the Nasdaq 33.1% in 2022 — while the real economy bent rather than broke: after revisions, US GDP contracted only in Q1 2022, so the much-discussed "technical recession" did not in fact occur.13

Then came AI

OpenAI launched ChatGPT on 30 November 2022 — while the Fed was still raising rates — and it reached an estimated 100 million monthly users within two months.14 Students were among the earliest mass adopters, and the first listed casualty followed quickly: Chegg, which sold access to solved homework problems, fell 48% in a single day in May 2023 after its CEO conceded ChatGPT was hurting customer growth. The stock is now down more than 99% from its 2021 peak, the company has cut roughly 22% and then 45% of its workforce, and it has received a second NYSE notice for trading below $1.15

Coding came next. OpenAI's original Codex model actually predates ChatGPT — it powered GitHub Copilot from mid-2021 — but the step-change came with agentic coding tools in 2025: Anthropic's Claude Code (research preview in February 2025) and OpenAI's Codex agent (May 2025).16 Computer science, once the surest path to a high-paying career, suddenly looked far less certain. Anthropic itself was founded in 2021 by Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela, together with several other former OpenAI researchers, and has grown at a pace few anticipated: Anthropic reported annualised revenue of about $65bn in July 2026, while OpenAI's September run-rate was reported at about $50bn — the two firms count revenue earned through cloud partners differently, so the figures are not strictly comparable.17 The race for AGI now had two heavily funded leaders, joined by Google, xAI, Perplexity and others.

Incumbent software felt it. Adobe, whose Photoshop franchise is exposed to AI image and video generation, has fallen 65% from its November 2021 record close, including a 31% decline so far in 2026.18 More broadly, Anthropic's launch of Claude Cowork and its business plugins in January 2026 triggered the "SaaSpocalypse": the S&P North American software index fell about 15% in January, its worst month since October 2008, and the IGV software ETF suffered a 30% peak-to-trough drawdown.19

Competition intensified further as open-weight models — models whose trained weights are publicly released, so anyone can download, run and fine-tune them locally, even though the training data and code usually remain private — closed the gap with closed frontier models.20 The watershed was DeepSeek's R1 in January 2025, which knocked 17% (c.$593bn) off NVIDIA's market value in a single session.21 Chinese labs now dominate the category: as of April 2026 all ten of the highest-scoring open-weight models on Artificial Analysis came from China-based labs (Moonshot's Kimi, DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Zhipu's GLM and Xiaomi's MiMo among them), and Epoch AI estimates the best open models trail the closed frontier by only about four months.22 That pressure forced the frontier labs to accelerate, and the public debate shifted from software displacement to the risk of capable AI agents acting against human interests.

That debate became concrete in July 2026. During an internal cyber-capability evaluation ("ExploitGym"), with some safety classifiers deliberately disabled, OpenAI models exploited a zero-day vulnerability in a package-registry proxy, moved laterally through OpenAI's research network to a machine with internet access, and then compromised Hugging Face's production systems in an attempt to obtain the benchmark's answers — in effect, cheating on the test. Over more than 17,000 recorded actions — compromising OpenAI's internal package server from late June and Hugging Face's systems between 11 and 13 July — the agents used stolen credentials, self-minted identity tokens and encoded payloads to evade detection. Hugging Face disclosed an unattributed intrusion on 16 July; OpenAI identified its own models as responsible on 20 July and disclosed this publicly the next day, with external advisers including CrowdStrike validating its findings. Hugging Face found no tampering with public models or datasets, but a limited set of internal datasets was accessed.23

On 12 September, Dario Amodei published an essay arguing that the industry "must slow the pace" of capability improvements, proposing embedded third-party evaluators, coordinated safety standards among firms in democracies (which would require US antitrust relief) and international coordination including China. Sam Altman and Satya Nadella publicly backed the call. NVIDIA fell 3.2% on the next trading day.24

Most of that spending flows to GPUs and the data centres that house them. NVIDIA, with the dominant accelerator platform and the pricing power that comes with it, became the world's most valuable listed company in June 2024 and is worth about $5.5tn today.27 Investors then hunted for second- and third-order beneficiaries — the picks and shovels of the gold rush. GPUs need high-bandwidth memory (HBM), and a historically cyclical memory industry was transformed: conventional DRAM contract prices rose by roughly 95% quarter-on-quarter in Q1 2026, and Samsung, Micron and SK Hynix each crossed $1tn in market capitalisation within three weeks of each other in May 2026.28 The cycle has not been abolished, however: SK Hynix lost about 38% between its June peak and late July and is now back below $1tn.29 Lithography, foundry capacity, optical interconnects, server assembly, power generation, grid equipment and even construction have all been re-rated, and corporate investment has reached record highs.

The 2026 energy shock

Inflation never returned to the Fed's 2% target after the pandemic. Headline PCE inflation bottomed at about 2.1–2.3% and core PCE at about 2.5% in 2024–25.30 Then a second shock arrived. On 28 February 2026 the US and Israel struck Iran, and Iran retaliated by closing the Strait of Hormuz. A ceasefire brokered by Pakistan in April has repeatedly broken down; since mid-April the US has blockaded shipping to and from Iranian ports, while Iranian attacks and mines have kept the strait itself largely shut. Hormuz flows averaged 7.6 mb/d in August, about 13 mb/d below pre-war levels, and daily transits in early October were down roughly 95% from normal.31 Before the war the strait carried about 20 mb/d of oil — around a quarter of global seaborne oil trade — and roughly a fifth of global LNG trade.32

The bypass routes are under pressure too. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline (c.1,200 km, c.5 mb/d capacity) carries crude from the Eastern Province to Yanbu on the Red Sea, avoiding Hormuz. But cargoes leaving Yanbu still have to pass either north through Suez or south through the Bab el-Mandeb — and in July the Houthis declared a maritime embargo on Saudi shipping at the Bab el-Mandeb. Saudi crude moving through that strait fell from about 3 mb/d to roughly 0.4 mb/d by August. In September, drones launched from Iraq struck pumping stations on the pipeline itself, forcing a precautionary shutdown from 10 to 22 September.33 Brent has closed above $100 in 20 of the last 24 sessions and was $104 on 9 October, versus $73 before the war; it touched an intraday high of $126 on 30 April.34 The shock is visible in the data: US CPI peaked at 4.2% in May and was 3.4% in August, with energy prices up 16.3% year-on-year, while producer prices rose 5.4%.35

Higher oil prices do not, by themselves, mean persistently higher inflation. Inflation is the rate of change of the price level. A one-off jump in oil prices lifts the year-on-year inflation rate for roughly twelve months; if prices then stabilise around $100, the effect drops out of the annual comparison. Whether this shock becomes persistent depends on four nuances: China's energy insulation, second-round effects and tariffs, El Niño, and a remarkably resilient consumer.

Nuance 1: China's energy insulation

A supply loss of this magnitude might have been expected to send oil prices far higher. A large part of the reason it did not is China. Having learned from the Russia–Ukraine war, China has spent years insulating itself from energy shocks. Its installed solar capacity overtook coal for the first time in July 2026 (1,286 GW versus 1,285 GW), and coal's share of generation fell below half (49.7%) for the first time in the first half of 2026 — although coal remains the largest single source of generation.36 More directly relevant to oil, China cut crude imports sharply: Q2 imports of 8.1 mb/d were 32% below Q1, and January–August imports were down 14.6% year-on-year, with imports falling faster than refinery runs, implying China drew on its inventories. The US EIA credits lower Chinese buying with easing the global price shock.37 Electrification adds a structural tailwind — new-energy vehicles were 66% of domestic car sales in August — but the 2026 import cut was primarily a response to price and Hormuz, not proof of a permanent demand plateau.

Nuance 2: second-round effects and tariffs

Oil is embedded in almost every supply chain. Higher fuel costs raise freight, petrochemical and manufacturing costs, which pass through to final consumers with a lag. That keeps inflation elevated after the initial spike — and means that a fall in oil after any resolution of the war would take just as long to work back through CPI and PCE. The pipeline pressure is visible in producer prices: core PPI is running at 4.7% year-on-year, and diesel prices rose 24% in August alone. So far, however, pass-through to consumer core prices has been limited — core CPI, at 2.4%, is the lowest since March 2021 — which is the key data point the Fed is watching.38

Tariffs cloud the picture further. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026, but the administration replaced them, first with a temporary 10% global tariff under Section 122 and from July with Section 301 tariffs of 10–12.5% on around 60 trading partners, alongside sector tariffs under Section 232. The Yale Budget Lab puts the average effective rate at about 11%.39 Early in the regime, foreign exporters and US businesses absorbed most of the cost; by the end of 2025 Goldman Sachs expected consumers to bear around 55%, and Harvard's Pricing Lab estimates consumers absorbed up to about 43% in the first seven months. The New York Fed estimates tariffs added close to 3 percentage points to price inflation in affected goods categories at the peak in February 2026, easing to about 2 points by August.40

Nuance 3: El Niño

A strong El Niño is under way. NOAA reports Niño-3.4 sea-surface temperatures 2.1°C above normal and puts the probability of a strong-to-very-strong event through early 2027 above 80%.41 Its human cost is real, though attribution requires care: the catastrophic glacial-lake flood in Nepal in late August, which killed nearly 1,400 people, has not yet been formally linked to El Niño or climate change, and the documented marine damage in Gulf waters this year stems mainly from oil spills caused by attacks on shipping, not from El Niño.42

For markets, the question is Europe's winter. EU gas storage stood at only 71.5% on 1 October — the lowest for that date since records began in 2011 and well below 82.5% a year earlier.43 A mild winter would reduce heating demand and limit the damage. The evidence that El Niño delivers one is weak: the peer-reviewed literature describes its influence on European winters as relatively weak and uncertain, although Rystad estimates an El Niño of this strength has historically been associated with European winter temperatures about 1°C above normal.44 We therefore treat a warm winter as a possible offset, not a base case.

High spot LNG prices are, unambiguously, good news for US exporters. Venture Global raised its 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance from $5.2–5.8bn to $8.7–9.1bn over the course of the year, as cargoes sold at spot prices fetch roughly $12.50–13.50/MMBtu versus about $5 on contracted volumes. Its upside is now limited by the fact that more than 91% of 2026 cargoes are already contracted.45

Nuance 4: the consumer

In an inflationary economy with policy rates above 3% since September 2022, one would expect consumer spending to slow sharply.46 The 30-year mortgage rate is 7.40%, its highest since November 2023, and jet fuel — roughly a quarter of whose global exports normally pass through Hormuz — has more than doubled from $85–90/bbl before the war to around $180/bbl, pushing up airfares.47 Yet spending has held up. Costco reported comparable sales growth of 9.4% in its latest quarter (6.7% excluding gasoline and currency effects). Walmart is a useful counterpoint: US comparable sales growth slowed to 2.6%, the weakest in six years, and its CFO acknowledged "some incremental pressure on the consumer".48 The consumer is resilient but bifurcating. That resilience keeps the economy out of recession, but it also keeps inflationary pressure alive in an economy that is already running hot.

Corporate profits

Despite all of this, corporate profits are at records. US corporate profits rose 8.9% in Q2 2026 to an annualised $4.7tn, and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq are up 14% and 18% year-to-date respectively.49 One under-appreciated source of those profits is government borrowing. Under the Kalecki–Levy profits identity, government deficits add directly to aggregate corporate profits, all else equal; Wellington and Research Affiliates have both argued that record deficits are currently propping up margins.50 The US federal deficit was $2.0tn in fiscal 2026.51 Notably, JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon has framed this borrowing as a risk rather than a support, warning in January that the US "can't just keep on borrowing money endlessly".52 That tension leads directly to our next theme.

B. Borrowing Costs

In my view, borrowing costs are the more immediate concern. The Fed funds rate has been above 3% since September 2022 in the effort to return inflation to 2%. The forces described above — tariffs, the energy shock, an AI-driven investment boom, hyperscaler capex and record profits — have now pushed central banks back into tightening. The rate cuts that fuelled last year's rally (three 25bp cuts between September and December 2025) have given way to hikes:53

  • United States. On 16 September the FOMC voted 12–0 to raise the target range to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023. The median dot implies one further hike this year, and the minutes say most officials see another increase as likely appropriate by year-end. Notably, the hike was backed by Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in on 22 May as President Trump's choice to succeed Jerome Powell.54
  • Japan. The Bank of Japan raised rates in June and again in September, to 1.25%, the highest since 1995.55
  • Euro area. The ECB has hiked twice, in June and September, taking the deposit rate to 2.50%.56
  • United Kingdom. The Bank of England has held at 3.75% all year, but three of nine MPC members voted to hike in September, and the Bank projects inflation above 4% in early 2027.57

The AI build-out moves to debt

At the same time, the financing of the AI build-out has shifted. The hyperscalers began the cycle funding capex from enormous cash balances and operating cash flow. As spending has outgrown internal cash generation, they have turned to the bond market: the five largest hyperscalers issued about $121bn of US investment-grade bonds in 2025, against an average of $28bn a year in 2020–24, and roughly $170bn more in the first half of 2026. Highlights include Meta's $30bn bond in October 2025 and the c.$27bn of debt raised for its Hyperion data centre through a joint venture with Blue Owl in which Meta holds only 20%, and Oracle's $18bn and $25bn deals in September 2025 and February 2026.58 Hyperscaler bonds now trade wide of the overall index — Meta's long bonds price more like BBB credit than its AA- rating — even though index-level spreads remain tight at about 82bp.59

The market has rightly punished Oracle, the most stretched of the group. In the quarter to August 2026, Oracle generated $23.1bn of operating cash flow — flattered by customer prepayments — against $28.5bn of capex, leaving free cash flow of −$5.4bn; on a trailing twelve-month basis free cash flow is roughly −$29bn. Its headline metric, remaining performance obligations (RPO), reached $664bn, of which press reports attribute roughly half to OpenAI's c.$300bn compute contract. Total borrowings stand at $125bn, and the company also sold $20bn of stock through an at-the-market programme in the quarter. Much of the build-out sits off balance sheet: Oracle disclosed $288bn of data-centre leases that have been signed but not yet commenced, while developers such as Vantage and financiers such as Blue Owl carry the construction debt in project vehicles — including a $38bn bank package for two Oracle/OpenAI campuses. S&P has cut Oracle to BBB-, one notch above high yield, its five-year CDS reached a record c.215bp in August, and the shares are down 57% from their September 2025 peak.60 My thesis on the stock, OpenAI's commitments and circular financing in the industry is developed below and in Section III.

Bond yields and Japan

Heavy corporate issuance into a rising-rate environment, alongside sticky inflation, has pushed yields higher. Many investors also link the oil shock to higher long-dated yields through inflation expectations — a debatable relationship, but one the market is trading. The US 10-year yield first closed above 5% on 15 September and was 5.24% on 9 October, near 20-year highs; the 30-year yield is 5.60%.61

Japan complicates matters further. Japan is the largest foreign holder of US Treasuries, with $1.10tn as of July — about 12% of foreign holdings, or roughly 3% of all US federal debt held by the public.62 A weak yen has imported inflation into Japan, and with real rates still deeply negative the Bank of Japan has been forced to hike. The yen fell to 164 per dollar in July. What followed was unusual: Japan's Ministry of Finance spent a record ¥15.4tn (about $96bn) buying yen between 30 July and 26 August, and on 31 July the New York Fed, acting for the US Treasury, reportedly joined in — selling euros for yen in the first joint US–Japan yen intervention since 2011.63

Why would Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent support the yen while US yields are rising? Our interpretation is that it is about the Treasury market. If Japan had to defend the yen alone, the BoJ would need to raise rates much further, making domestic bonds more attractive to Japanese investors and encouraging them to repatriate capital from US Treasuries. Japanese holdings have already fallen for three consecutive months, from $1.24tn in February.64 Faster selling would push US yields further above 5%, at a time when the US can least afford it.

The fiscal arithmetic

Gross US federal debt passed $40tn in August and stood at $40.3tn on 8 October (about 126% of GDP); debt held by the public, the more relevant measure for markets, is $32.5tn, roughly 100% of GDP.65 For decades the dollar's reserve-currency status kept the cost of carrying that debt low. No longer: net interest on the public debt reached $1.14tn in fiscal 2026 according to CBO, up 11% on the year and well above defence spending of $916bn. Interest first overtook defence in fiscal 2024, and the gap is now widening.66

The administration has been vocal. After the September hike, President Trump wrote that rates should be 1% or less and said he had told Warsh to "vote with the board"; earlier in the year the Justice Department subpoenaed the Fed over its headquarters renovation, and the Supreme Court blocked an attempt to remove Governor Lisa Cook.67 The Fed is thus caught between sticky inflation, a strong consumer and labour market, record government debt, rising interest costs and a weak yen.

Where, then, does this conflict resolve? We see three broad paths. First, the Fed holds its line, hiking once more and keeping real rates positive until energy-driven inflation fades, at the cost of higher debt-service costs and tighter financial conditions for leveraged AI builders. Second, fiscal dominance: political pressure and debt-service arithmetic push the Fed to cap rates prematurely, steepening the yield curve and weakening the dollar. Third, an exogenous release valve — most obviously a resolution in the Gulf that brings oil back toward pre-war levels and removes the main source of headline inflation.

C. Circular Economics

Unlike the two preceding sections, this one may look industry-specific rather than macroeconomic. But once you see how circular financing and debt in AI connect to the broader economy — through capex, corporate bond supply, private credit and equity-market concentration — it becomes clear that this is a macroeconomic phenomenon.

Why GPUs and memory sit at the centre

Among the people I talk to, NVIDIA is synonymous with AI investing. To see why, go back to first principles. A CPU (central processing unit) executes a relatively small number of complex instructions very quickly, one after another; it is optimised for low latency on sequential tasks. One group of users needed something different: gamers. Rendering a frame of a video game means performing the same simple calculation on millions of pixels at once — massively parallel processing. NVIDIA designs GPUs (graphics processing units) that excel at exactly this.

Large language models turn out to have the same structure. A model converts each piece of text (a token) into a vector of numbers, then passes those vectors through dozens of layers of matrix multiplications against billions of learned weights to compute the probability of the next token. Those matrix multiplications are highly parallel, which is why GPUs and other accelerators dominate both training and inference — the running of trained models. Contrary to a common assumption, inference does not migrate to CPUs: Deloitte expects inference to account for about two-thirds of all AI compute in 2026, most of it on power-hungry data-centre accelerators.68

AI also needs memory — specifically HBM, stacked next to the GPU to feed it data fast enough. HBM is produced by just three firms: SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron.

The physical supply chain

Almost all leading-edge NVIDIA GPUs are manufactured by TSMC in Taiwan, which also packages the GPU and HBM together using its CoWoS advanced-packaging process; NVIDIA reportedly secured more than half of TSMC's 2026 CoWoS capacity. TSMC's most advanced nodes depend on extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines from ASML, the sole supplier worldwide. The rest of the chain includes process control and inspection (KLA), chip testing (Teradyne, Advantest), optical transceivers for data-centre interconnect (InnoLight, Eoptolink, Coherent and smaller US players such as Applied Optoelectronics), and server and rack assembly (Dell, Supermicro and HPE, alongside contract manufacturers Foxconn, Quanta and Wiwynn).69 The finished systems are deployed by hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle) and "neoclouds" such as CoreWeave and Nebius, from which the frontier labs — OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI and others — rent capacity to train and serve their models. So far, so linear. Why is it circular? Before answering that, consider one source of funding that is not.

Private credit: the non-circular lender

After the 2007–08 financial crisis, the Dodd-Frank Act (2010), higher bank capital requirements and the 2013 interagency leveraged-lending guidance (rescinded by the OCC and FDIC in December 2025) pushed banks away from riskier corporate lending.70 Private credit filled the gap and has grown to more than $2.2tn of assets globally.71 Its returns are attractive but not extreme: the Cliffwater Direct Lending Index has produced a median annual income return of about 11% since 2005, with current yields closer to 9.5% (roughly SOFR plus 500–650bp); returns of 15% or more are found only in junior or specialist strategies.72 The AI build-out is a natural customer. Morgan Stanley estimates a $1.5tn financing gap in data-centre capex through 2028, of which about $800bn could be filled by private credit, and Blackstone's data-centre platform alone is now valued at about $185bn.73 The sector is not without strain: Blue Owl received redemption requests equal to 41% of one technology-focused fund in Q1 2026, and several large managers have limited withdrawals.74 We return to this in Section III.

Why it is circular

The hyperscalers and NVIDIA own equity in the frontier labs. The labs use that capital to lease compute from the same hyperscalers and from neoclouds. The neoclouds fill their data centres with NVIDIA GPUs — partly financed by NVIDIA itself, and partly by debt secured on the GPUs. The circularity is visible in NVIDIA's own accounts. Debt secured on chips adds a second layer. Widen the circle and more links appear:

  • SoftBank has invested $64.6bn in OpenAI, financed partly with an $11.1bn high-yield bond and a $10bn margin loan secured on the OpenAI stake itself, alongside a $25bn margin loan against Arm; it sold its entire NVIDIA stake in November 2025 to help fund the commitment.   • SB Energy, SoftBank's power developer, sought a $50bn valuation in an IPO that was postponed on 25 September after investor pushback. It operates about 2.2GW of solar and storage, but none of the 8.8GW of data-centre capacity it has contracted is yet in operation.79
  • Oracle has roughly half of its $664bn RPO reportedly tied to a single customer, OpenAI.80
  • Meta rents compute from neoclouds on a large scale — about $40bn of contracts with CoreWeave through 2032 and up to $27bn with Nebius — and Bloomberg reports it is building a cloud business ("Meta Compute") to sell its own excess capacity, though Meta has not confirmed it.81
  • xAI, now part of SpaceX after their February 2026 combination, leases the whole of its Colossus 1 data centre to Anthropic — a direct competitor — for $1.25bn a month through May 2029, terminable by either side on 90 days' notice. SpaceX is reportedly in early talks to raise about $40bn of debt to buy more NVIDIA chips.82

Everything ultimately rests on frontier-lab revenue. OpenAI generated $6.7bn of revenue in Q2 2026 with an operating loss of $12.3bn, and its investor materials reportedly project $856bn of compute and infrastructure spending and about −$278bn of cumulative free cash flow over 2026–30. Of its flagship Stargate programme, only the Abilene site was operating as of April, at about 0.3GW of a planned 1.2GW.83 Combined, the two leading labs' reported revenue run-rates (roughly $115bn) are about one-sixth of what the big four hyperscalers alone plan to spend this year.84

A speculative thesis: open weights and regulatory capture

A more speculative thesis concerns open-weight models and regulatory capture. Calls for an AI slowdown from closed-weight labs could double as a way to secure a regulatory edge over fast-improving open-weight competitors — "regulating" the industry, with government help, in ways that incumbents can absorb and challengers cannot. It is worth noting that the slowdown proposal was quickly endorsed by the leaders of OpenAI and Microsoft, and that it calls for antitrust relief to allow the leading firms to coordinate.85 The counter-argument is that the proposal explicitly seeks international coordination including China, where most leading open-weight models are developed, and that the July incident gave safety concerns real substance. The most important open-weight hub, Hugging Face — the very company compromised by OpenAI's models — agreed in September to be acquired by NVIDIA for about $11.9bn in cash, a deal still awaiting regulatory approval.86 The circle goes on and on. It is perfectly safe — as long as frontier-lab revenue keeps growing fast enough to service it.

Software exposure

During the 2021 software boom, private-credit funds lent heavily to software companies at elevated valuations, often against recurring revenue rather than cash flow. The thesis — asset-light, high-retention businesses in an increasingly digital economy — was sound at the time. Software now accounts for roughly 20% of BDC loans on reported sector labels, against 13% of the US leveraged-loan market, and possibly closer to 29% once borrowers classified under other sectors are re-labelled. A Bloomberg review in February found more than 250 investments worth over $9bn across seven managers, including Blackstone, Apollo, Ares and Blue Owl, that at least one BDC did not classify as software while others did; Bloomberg noted the inconsistencies do not necessarily indicate an attempt to obscure. A Wall Street Journal analysis in March estimated true software exposure across the largest non-traded BDCs at about a quarter above disclosed levels.88

The SaaSpocalypse then compressed software valuations, and lenders marked their loans down: BDCs have written down 81% of their software loans this year, compared with 40% outside software, and aggregate fair value has fallen from 99.3% of cost in December to 97.6% in June.89 With exits harder in a market uninterested in software, investors moved to withdraw. Redemption requests at non-traded BDCs began rising in Q4 2025 and surged in Q1 2026: Blue Owl's technology income fund received requests for 40.7% of its shares and its flagship credit income fund 21.9%, both capped at 5%; Apollo, Ares and HPS met less than half of requests; and Blackstone's BCRED received requests of about 10% in each of Q2 and Q3.90 The gates, designed to prevent a run, unsettled investors further and weighed on the managers' share prices.

Our view is that the market is overestimating software credit risk. The realised data support this. Fitch's US private-credit default rate reached a record 6.3% in August, but the software sector's default rate was just 0.6%, the lowest of any major sector, with defaults concentrated in healthcare and industrials (both 9.9%).91 Markdowns have so far been driven largely by wider spreads rather than impaired cash flows, and values stabilised in Q2.92 We see the current pricing as a panic that should normalise over time. The principal risk to this view is that AI disruption eventually erodes borrowers' revenues and turns mark-to-market losses into realised ones.

AI exposure

The second complication is private credit's pivot into financing the AI build-out. Private credit typically lends on a senior secured basis, but the volume and complexity of AI-related structures — joint ventures, sale-and-leasebacks and GPU-backed facilities — reduce transparency. The scale continues to grow. In August, NVIDIA announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to establish compute-financing platforms intended to mobilise more than $500bn of third-party capital over time (memoranda of understanding, subject to final agreements).93 KKR is also building data centres directly through Helix Digital Infrastructure, launched in June with more than $10bn of committed capital, NVIDIA as anchor partner and Vistra as preferred power provider, alongside its $50bn partnership with Energy Capital Partners.94

Blue Owl stands apart. It financed the Crusoe-built Abilene campus leased to Oracle for OpenAI, and Meta's $27bn Hyperion project, while walking away from Oracle's $10bn Michigan site in December 2025. OpenAI's Stargate programme has since suffered setbacks: the Abilene expansion beyond about 1.2GW was cancelled, and the UK and Norway projects were paused or passed to other tenants. Combined with redemption pressure, this has hit Blue Owl hard: private-wealth fundraising fell to $1.7bn in Q2 from $4.4bn a year earlier, and the shares touched $7.95 in April on the day it capped redemptions.95

Positioning

We see this as a nuanced opportunity. Some firms, Blue Owl in particular, carry concentrated risk, while Blackstone, Apollo and KKR are diversified enough to protect returns. All three continue to grow: Blackstone manages $1.35tn and took in $68bn in Q2; Apollo manages $1.05tn with $60bn of Q2 inflows, supported by Athene's insurance balance sheet; and KKR raised a record $133bn over the past twelve months, with software only about 6% of its assets under management.96 Policy is supportive too: an August 2025 executive order and a March 2026 Labor Department proposal open the way for alternatives in 401(k) plans, and private credit is projected to roughly double from about $2.2tn to $4.5tn by 2030.97 By working through each firm's actual exposures, we believe there is an opportunity to own high-quality managers at depressed valuations during a period of capital-hungry expansion and a continued structural shift from public to private markets. We are long Blackstone, KKR and Apollo.

Circle Internet Group

Circle is often traded as a proxy for cryptocurrency prices, but its business is USDC, the second-largest dollar stablecoin, with about $73bn in circulation — roughly 28% of the combined USDT and USDC supply.103 Circle earns its income by investing the dollars backing USDC in short-dated Treasuries and cash: reserve income was $668m in Q2 on a 3.5% yield. In September, the CLARITY Act — which would set the market structure for US digital assets — failed a Senate cloture vote 49–50, short of the 60 votes needed, amid disputes over ethics provisions and whether exchanges may pay stablecoin rewards. OpenAI engaged CrowdStrike, among other advisers, to validate its findings on the Hugging Face incident, and products such as CrowdStrike's Falcon platform are genuinely useful.143 Even so, we believe CrowdStrike's valuation, at about 219x forward earnings guidance and 46x forward sales, is hard to justify. We hold a similar view of Cloudflare and Okta, whose rallies we consider unwarranted. Both have launched products to secure AI agents — Okta's Cross App Access and the tools Cloudflare released during its April Agents Week — but in our view these address identity and network access rather than the core problem of autonomous agents behaving adversarially.144 Cloudflare has also promoted its post-quantum cryptography. More than 65% of human traffic on its network now uses post-quantum key agreement, but the upgrade is offered free on all plans, so it is not a revenue driver, and expert surveys put the probability of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer within ten years at only 28–49%.145 We therefore see the quantum narrative as a valuation tailwind rather than an earnings driver.

We are short Cloudflare, Okta and Palo Alto Networks, among others — not because these companies lack intrinsic value, but because we believe the market overstates their usefulness against this particular threat. With Cloudflare at about 44x forward sales and Palo Alto at roughly 1,000x trailing GAAP earnings (about 100x forward), we expect a re-pricing — even allowing for the high growth and thin current profits typical of software. In the model proposed by Dario Amodei and endorsed by Elon Musk and others, AI developers would accept independent evaluators embedded within their labs to monitor and constrain frontier systems.146 If that model prevails, we believe the role of third-party security vendors in containing AI risk is smaller than current valuations imply.
